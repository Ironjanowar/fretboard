# Chord Progressions Catalog for Fretboard App

## Overview

Comprehensive catalog of chord progressions organized by category, designed to populate a "Chord Progressions" selector in the Fretboard guitar visualizer app. Each progression specifies:

- **Name**: Display identifier
- **Degrees**: Roman numeral formula (using standard notation: uppercase = major, lowercase = minor, ° = diminished, + = augmented)
- **Key Mode**: Whether it's typically used in major, minor, or both
- **Genre/Tradition**: Musical style or tradition it belongs to
- **Example**: Example chords in a specific key
- **Notable Songs**: Well-known songs that use it
- **Scale Source**: Which scale the diatonic chords come from (for app integration)

## A) Famous / Classic Progressions

### 1. Pop Punk / Axis of Awesome
- **Name**: "Pop: I-V-vi-IV"
- **Degrees**: I-V-vi-IV
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Pop, pop-punk, rock
- **Example (C)**: C-G-Am-F
- **Notable Songs**: "Don't Stop Believin'" (Journey), "Let It Be" (verse, Beatles), "No Woman No Cry" (Bob Marley), "I'm Yours" (Jason Mraz), "Despacito" (Luis Fonsi)
- **Notes**: Famously popularized by "Axis of Awesome" comedy medley showing dozens of hit songs use this exact progression

### 2. Classic Three-Chord
- **Name**: "Classic: I-IV-V"
- **Degrees**: I-IV-V
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Rock, blues, country, folk
- **Example (G)**: G-C-D
- **Notable Songs**: "Wild Thing" (The Troggs), "La Bamba" (Ritchie Valens), "Twist and Shout" (Beatles), "Rock Around the Clock" (Bill Haley)
- **Notes**: The foundational rock/blues progression. Endlessly reusable; basis of 12-bar blues

### 3. 12-Bar Blues
- **Name**: "Blues: 12-Bar (I-IV-V)"
- **Degrees**: I-I-I-I-IV-IV-I-I-V-IV-I-I (extended form)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Blues, rock, jazz
- **Example (A)**: A-A-A-A-D-D-A-A-E-D-A-A
- **Notable Songs**: "Sweet Home Chicago" (Blues Brothers), "Pride and Joy" (Stevie Ray Vaughan), "Johnny B. Goode" (Chuck Berry), "Red House" (Jimi Hendrix)
- **Notes**: The quintessential blues form; 12 bars with specific pattern of I, IV, V chords

### 4. Jazz ii-V-I
- **Name**: "Jazz: ii-V-I"
- **Degrees**: ii-V-I
- **Key Mode**: Major (also minor: ii°-V-i)
- **Genre**: Jazz
- **Example (C)**: Dm7-G7-Cmaj7
- **Notable Songs**: "Autumn Leaves," "All The Things You Are," "Tune Up" (Eddie Vinson / Miles Davis)
- **Notes**: The most important progression in jazz; appears in virtually every jazz standard. Often extended with 7th chords

### 5. 50s / Doo-Wop
- **Name**: "50s: I-vi-IV-V"
- **Degrees**: I-vi-IV-V
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Doo-wop, 1950s pop, early rock & roll
- **Example (C)**: C-Am-F-G
- **Notable Songs**: "Earth Angel" (The Penguins), "Stand By Me" (Ben E. King), "Everyday" (Buddy Holly), "Duke of Earl" (Gene Chandler)
- **Notes**: Also known as the "50s progression" or "doo-wop progression"

### 6. Pachelbel / Circle of Fifths
- **Name**: "Classical: Pachelbel (I-V-vi-iii-IV-I-IV-V)"
- **Degrees**: I-V-vi-iii-IV-I-IV-V
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Classical, baroque, pop (frequent borrowing)
- **Example (C)**: C-G-Am-Em-F-C-F-G
- **Notable Songs**: "Canon in D" (Pachelbel — in D: D-A-Bm-F#m-G-D-G-A), "Basket Case" (Green Day), "Graduation (Friends Forever)" (Vitamin C)
- **Notes**: Based on Pachelbel's Canon. The descending-fifth movement creates a smooth, satisfying circular harmonic motion

### 7. Pop vi-IV-I-V (Sensitive Female Chord Progression)
- **Name**: "Pop: vi-IV-I-V"
- **Degrees**: vi-IV-I-V
- **Key Mode**: Major (starts on relative minor)
- **Genre**: Pop, adult contemporary
- **Example (C)**: Am-F-C-G
- **Notable Songs**: "Apologize" (OneRepublic), "Glycerine" (Bush), "Save Tonight" (Eagle Eye Cherry), "Zombie" (The Cranberries)
- **Notes**: Same four chords as I-V-vi-IV but starting on vi, giving a more melancholic, yearning quality. Called "sensitive female chord progression" by Boston Globe

### 8. Mixolydian Rock
- **Name**: "Rock: I-bVII-IV"
- **Degrees**: I-bVII-IV
- **Key Mode**: Major (mixolydian)
- **Genre**: Rock, folk-rock
- **Example (D)**: D-C-G
- **Notable Songs**: "Sweet Home Alabama" (Lynyrd Skynyrd), "A Hard Day's Night" (Beatles intro), "With or Without You" (U2, partial), "Sympathy for the Devil" (Rolling Stones)
- **Notes**: Uses the flattened 7th degree (bVII) from mixolydian mode; quintessential rock sound

### 9. Folk Vamp / Two-Chord
- **Name**: "Folk: I-IV"
- **Degrees**: I-IV
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Folk, rock, pop, drone music
- **Example (E)**: E-A
- **Notable Songs**: "Born in the U.S.A." (Bruce Springsteen, verse), "Mellowship Slinky in B Major" (RHCP intro), many folk and drone-based songs
- **Notes**: Simplest common progression. Creates an open, unresolved feeling. Often used as a vamp

### 10. Minor Pop (i-VI-III-VII)
- **Name**: "Pop: i-VI-III-VII"
- **Degrees**: i-VI-III-VII
- **Key Mode**: Minor (natural minor / aeolian)
- **Genre**: Pop, EDM, dance
- **Example (Am)**: Am-F-C-G
- **Notable Songs**: "Wake Me Up" (Avicii), "Rolling in the Deep" (Adele), "Don't You Worry Child" (Swedish House Mafia), "Let Her Go" (Passenger)
- **Notes**: The minor-key equivalent of the vi-IV-I-V pop progression. Extremely common in modern pop and EDM

### 11. Minor Pop Variant (i-bVI-bIII-bVII)
- **Name**: "Pop: i-bVI-bIII-bVII"
- **Degrees**: i-bVI-bIII-bVII
- **Key Mode**: Minor
- **Genre**: Pop, rock
- **Example (Am)**: Am-F-Dm-G
- **Notable Songs**: "Mr. Brightside" (The Killers), "Disturbia" (Rihanna), "Stronger" (Kanye West)
- **Notes**: All-natural-minor diatonic chords; the most common minor-key four-chord loop

### 12. Canon Rock (V-i-VI-IV variant)
- **Name**: "Rock: V-i-VI-IV"
- **Degrees**: V-i-VI-IV (starting on V)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (harmonic minor flavor)
- **Genre**: Rock, instrumental guitar
- **Example (Am)**: E-Am-F-Dm
- **Notable Songs**: "Canon Rock" (JerryC / Funtwo), many Yngwie Malmsteen passages
- **Notes**: Fusion of Pachelbel's Canon with rock/metal aesthetics; uses the harmonic minor raised 7th

### 13. Pedal / Drone (I-vii°-I)
- **Name**: "Classical: I-vii°-I (Pedal)"
- **Degrees**: I-vii°-I
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Classical, folk
- **Example (C)**: C-B°-C
- **Notable Songs**: Various classical pedal points, folk drones
- **Notes**: Uses the leading-tone diminished as a neighbor chord; creates tension and release over a static tonic

## B) Curious / Interesting Progressions

### 14. Modal Interchange / Borrowed iv
- **Name**: "Modal Interchange: I-iv"
- **Degrees**: I-iv (borrowing iv from parallel minor)
- **Key Mode**: Major (with borrowed minor iv)
- **Genre**: Pop, rock, classical
- **Example (C)**: C-Fm
- **Notable Songs**: "Creep" (Radiohead — G-B-C-Cm), "In My Life" (Beatles), "Beethoven's Sonata Op. 13 (Pathétique)"
- **Notes**: Borrowing the iv chord from the parallel minor key creates an emotional, bittersweet color. One of the most common modal interchange techniques

### 15. Creep Progression (I-III-IV-iv)
- **Name**: "Modal Interchange: I-III-IV-iv"
- **Degrees**: I-III-IV-iv
- **Key Mode**: Major (with borrowed iv)
- **Genre**: Alternative rock, art rock
- **Example (G)**: G-B-C-Cm
- **Notable Songs**: "Creep" (Radiohead), "SexyBack" (Justin Timberlake), "Loser" (Beck, partial)
- **Notes**: The chromatic mediant I→III and the modal interchange IV→iv combine for a distinctive, melancholic-yet-grandiose sound. Iconically associated with Radiohead's "Creep"

### 16. Chromatic Mediant (I-bIII)
- **Name**: "Chromatic Mediant: I-bIII"
- **Degrees**: I-bIII
- **Key Mode**: Major (with bIII borrowed from parallel minor)
- **Genre**: Rock, film music, progressive
- **Example (C)**: C-Eb
- **Notable Songs**: "Where the Streets Have No Name" (U2 intro), film scores (John Williams, Danny Elfman), "Strawberry Fields Forever" (Beatles)
- **Notes**: Chromatic mediant relationship — roots a major third apart, both major chords. Creates a dramatic, cinematic quality

### 17. Chromatic Mediant (I-bVI)
- **Name**: "Chromatic Mediant: I-bVI"
- **Degrees**: I-bVI
- **Key Mode**: Major (with bVI from parallel minor)
- **Genre**: Rock, film music, classical
- **Example (C)**: C-Ab
- **Notable Songs**: "Yesterday" (partial turnaround, Beatles), film scores, "Dream On" (Aerosmith, partial)
- **Notes**: The bVI borrowed from parallel minor; creates a broad, expansive, sometimes heroic quality. Very common in film music

### 18. Chromatic Mediant (I-VI)
- **Name**: "Chromatic Mediant: I-VI"
- **Degrees**: I-VI
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, Broadway, classical
- **Example (C)**: C-A
- **Notable Songs**: Many show tunes and jazz standards, "Have You Met Miss Jones?" (bridge)
- **Notes**: Diatonic mediant; roots a major third apart. Less dramatic than the chromatic versions but still provides harmonic color

### 19. Chromatic Mediant (I-III)
- **Name**: "Chromatic Mediant: I-III"
- **Degrees**: I-III
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, classical, Broadway
- **Example (C)**: C-E
- **Notable Songs**: "Have You Met Miss Jones?" (Richard Rodgers), various Broadway transitions
- **Notes**: Roots a major third apart, both major. Bright, unexpected harmonic lift. Used in Coltrane changes

### 20. Neapolitan (bII)
- **Name**: "Classical: i-bII-V-i (Neapolitan)"
- **Degrees**: i-bII-V-i (or i-bII-V7-i)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (primarily; sometimes major)
- **Genre**: Classical, jazz
- **Example (Am)**: Am-Bb-E7-Am
- **Notable Songs**: Beethoven Piano Sonatas, Schubert songs, jazz standards with classical influence
- **Notes**: The Neapolitan chord (bII, a major chord on the lowered 2nd degree) creates a dramatic pre-dominant function. Most commonly in first inversion in classical music. In jazz, related to tritone substitution

### 21. Neapolitan in Major (IV-bII-I)
- **Name**: "Classical: IV-bII-I"
- **Degrees**: IV-bII-I
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Classical, jazz
- **Example (C)**: F-Db-C
- **Notable Songs**: Schubert, Chopin, various romantic-era pieces
- **Notes**: The Neapolitan (bII) used between IV and I creates a chromatic approach to the tonic; an unexpected, rich color

### 22. Descending Chromatic Bass (I-i7-IV-bvi°-I)
- **Name**: "Chromatic: Descending Bass (I-i7-IV-iv6-I)"
- **Degrees**: I-i7-IV-iv6-I (or variants with chromatic passing chords)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Pop, jazz, classical
- **Example (C)**: C-Cm7/B-F-Fm6/Ab-C
- **Notable Songs**: "Stairway to Heaven" (Led Zeppelin, partial), "My Funny Valentine" (jazz standard), "Chelsea Bridge" (Billy Strayhorn)
- **Notes**: Bass descends chromatically creating a sophisticated, emotive line. Very common in jazz ballads and classical music

### 23. Line Cliche / Descending Minor (i-bVII-bVI-V)
- **Name**: "Chromatic: Line Cliche (i-bVII-bVI-V)"
- **Degrees**: i-bVII-bVI-V (or i-bVII-bVI-bVII)
- **Key Mode**: Minor
- **Genre**: Jazz, pop, film
- **Example (Am)**: Am-G-F-E (or Am-G-F-E7)
- **Notable Songs**: "My Favorite Things" (Sound of Music), "Europa" (Santana), various James Bond themes, "Stairway to Heaven" (middle section)
- **Notes**: Chromatic descending line in the bass from i to V. The "James Bond" chord progression. Creates a cinematic, mysterious quality

### 24. Omnipotent Progression (I-VII-iv-iv°-III-II-I)
- **Name**: "Classical: Omnipotent (I-VII-iv-iv°-III-II-I)"
- **Degrees**: I-VII-iv-iv°-III-II-I (descending line with passing diminished)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (relative to a major tonic)
- **Genre**: Classical, Baroque
- **Example (Am relative: C)**: C-G-Am-A°-F-E-C (or in A minor: A-G-F#dim-E-Am)
- **Notable Songs**: Bach chorales, various Baroque works, "Stairway to Heaven" (partial descent)
- **Notes**: The "omnipotent progression" — a Baroque-era descending bass line using a passing diminished chord. One of the most important progressions in Western classical music history

### 25. Ascending Bass (I-ii-iii-IV)
- **Name**: "Pop: Ascending (I-ii-iii-IV)"
- **Degrees**: I-ii-iii-IV
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Pop, jazz
- **Example (C)**: C-Dm-Em-F
- **Notable Songs**: Various pop songs, "Lean on Me" (Bill Withers, partial), many jazz ballad intros
- **Notes**: Ascending stepwise motion through diatonic chords; creates a gentle, building, optimistic quality

### 26. Backdoor Progression (iv-bVII-I)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Backdoor (iv-bVII-I)"
- **Degrees**: iv-bVII7-I (or iv-bVII-I)
- **Key Mode**: Major (with borrowed minor iv and bVII)
- **Genre**: Jazz, soul
- **Example (C)**: Fm-Bb7-C
- **Notable Songs**: "Lady Bird" (Tadd Dameron), various jazz standards
- **Notes**: Approaches the tonic from the "back door" via the subdominant minor and its dominant (bVII). A staple of sophisticated jazz harmony. The bVII7 acts as the dominant of iv, resolving deceptively to I

### 27. Tritone Substitution (ii-bII-I)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Tritone Sub (ii-bII7-I)"
- **Degrees**: ii-bII7-I
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz
- **Example (C)**: Dm7-Db7-Cmaj7
- **Notable Songs**: "Misty," "Round Midnight" (Thelonious Monk), most bebop jazz standards
- **Notes**: Replaces V7 with bII7 (the tritone substitute). The bII7 shares the same tritone (3rd and 7th) as V7, creating a chromatic bass descent. One of the most important jazz substitutions

### 28. Secondary Dominant (I-V/ii-ii-V-I)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Secondary Dominant (I-V/ii-ii-V-I)"
- **Degrees**: I-V/ii-ii-V-I (e.g., I-A7-ii-V-I in C)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, Broadway
- **Example (C)**: Cmaj7-A7-Dm7-G7-Cmaj7
- **Notable Songs**: Various jazz standards, "I've Got Rhythm," many Broadway tunes
- **Notes**: Uses a secondary dominant (V/ii) to approach ii chromatically. Creates forward harmonic motion and chromatic interest

### 29. Chromatic Walkdown (I-bVII-VI-bVII-I)
- **Name**: "Rock: Chromatic Walkdown (I-bVII-VI-bVII-I)"
- **Degrees**: I-bVII-VI(b)-bVII-I
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Rock, pop
- **Example (A)**: A-G-F#m-G-A (or A-G-F-G-A with chromatic mediant)
- **Notable Songs**: "Hey Jude" (Beatles, outro section), various rock ballads
- **Notes**: Stepwise descent from I to bVI with a return through bVII; creates a dramatic, descending harmonic gesture

## C) Exotic / World Progressions

### 30. Andalusian Cadence / Flamenco
- **Name**: "Flamenco: Andalusian Cadence (i-bVII-bVI-V)"
- **Degrees**: i-bVII-bVI-V (or i-VII-VI-V)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (phrygian / aeolian)
- **Genre**: Flamenco, Spanish, classical, rock
- **Example (Am)**: Am-G-F-E (or E for the harmonic minor raised 7th)
- **Notable Songs**: "Hit the Road Jack" (Ray Charles), "California Dreamin'" (The Mamas & the Papas), "Stray Cat Strut" (Stray Cats), "Sultans of Swing" (Dire Straits, partial), "Runaway" (Del Shannon)
- **Notes**: The most famous flamenco progression. Descending bass line from i to V. The E major chord in A minor (harmonic minor raised 7th) provides the characteristic Spanish/flamenco tension. Also called the "minor descending tetrachord"

### 31. Flamenco / Phrygian Dominant (i-bII-bIII-bII)
- **Name**: "Flamenco: Phrygian Dominant (i-bII-bIII-bII)"
- **Degrees**: i-bII-bIII-bII (in phrygian dominant)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (phrygian dominant / harmonic minor)
- **Genre**: Flamenco, Middle Eastern
- **Example (E)**: E-F-G-F (in E phrygian dominant)
- **Notable Songs**: Various flamenco palos, Middle Eastern-influenced rock and metal
- **Notes**: Based on the phrygian dominant scale (5th mode of harmonic minor). The bII chord (F major over E tonic) creates the characteristic flamenco "bite." Often used as an ostinato

### 32. Harmonic Minor (i-iv-VII or i-iv-V)
- **Name**: "Harmonic Minor: i-iv-V"
- **Degrees**: i-iv-V (using harmonic minor raised 7th)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (harmonic minor)
- **Genre**: Classical, Eastern European, metal
- **Example (Am)**: Am-Dm-E7
- **Notable Songs**: Various classical pieces, klezmer, neo-classical metal (Yngwie Malmsteen)
- **Notes**: Uses the raised 7th (V instead of bVII) from harmonic minor for stronger dominant-tonic resolution. Characteristic of Eastern European and classical music

### 33. Harmonic Minor Full (i-iv-VII°-III-VI-ii°-V-i)
- **Name**: "Harmonic Minor: Full Cadence (iv-V-i)"
- **Degrees**: iv-V-i (harmonic minor cadence)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (harmonic minor)
- **Genre**: Classical, metal, world
- **Example (Am)**: Dm-E7-Am
- **Notable Songs**: Classical cadences, "House of the Rising Sun" (partial), various metal passages
- **Notes**: The authentic cadence in harmonic minor. The V7 (E7) contains the raised 7th (G#) creating strong pull to the tonic. Very common in classical and metal

### 34. Byzantine / Double Harmonic (I-bII-I-vII or i-bII-i-VII)
- **Name**: "Exotic: Byzantine / Double Harmonic (I-bII-I)"
- **Degrees**: I-bII-I (and variants like I-bII-iii-bII-I)
- **Key Mode**: Major or minor (double harmonic scale)
- **Genre**: Byzantine, Greek, Middle Eastern, Indian classical
- **Example (D)**: D-Eb-D (or D-Eb-F#-Eb-D for the full scale flavor)
- **Notable Songs**: "Miserlou" (Dick Dale), Greek and Middle Eastern traditional music, Bollywood
- **Notes**: Based on the double harmonic (Byzantine) scale with augmented 2nd intervals. The I-bII movement is the signature sound of this scale. "Miserlou" is the most famous Western example

### 35. Hungarian Minor (i-bII-iv or i-bVI-V)
- **Name**: "Exotic: Hungarian Minor (i-bII-iv)"
- **Degrees**: i-bII-iv (harmonic minor with raised 4th)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (Hungarian / gypsy minor)
- **Genre**: Hungarian, Eastern European, klezmer, gypsy jazz
- **Example (Am)**: Am-Bb-Dm (using the raised 4th, D natural)
- **Notable Songs**: Traditional Hungarian and Roma music, Brahms Hungarian Dances, Django Reinhardt gypsy jazz passages
- **Notes**: The Hungarian minor scale (harmonic minor with raised 4th) gives a distinctive Eastern European / gypsy flavor. The augmented 2nd between bIII and #IV is the signature interval

### 36. Japanese Pentatonic / Hirajoshi (I-IV-bV or I-bIII-IV)
- **Name**: "World: Japanese / Hirajoshi (I-IV-bVI)"
- **Degrees**: I-IV-bVI (or I-bIII-IV in hirajoshi)
- **Key Mode**: Major (pentatonic variants)
- **Genre**: Japanese traditional, ambient, world fusion
- **Example (C)**: C-F-Ab (using hirajoshi intervals)
- **Notable Songs**: Traditional Japanese koto and shamisen music, film scores referencing Japanese aesthetics
- **Notes**: The Hirajoshi and other Japanese pentatonic scales (In, Insen, Iwato) create distinctly Japanese harmonic colors. These scales often include seconds that sound "dissonant" to Western ears but are consonant in Japanese tradition. Limited chord options due to pentatonic nature — often used melodically over drone

### 37. Japanese Hirajoshi Scale Chords (I-bII-V-bVI)
- **Name**: "World: Hirajoshi Chords (I-bII-V-bVI)"
- **Degrees**: I-bII-V-bVI (limited triads from hirajoshi)
- **Key Mode**: Major (hirajoshi pentatonic)
- **Genre**: Japanese, ambient, film
- **Example (C)**: C-Db-G-Ab
- **Notable Songs**: Traditional Japanese music, Joe Hisaishi (Studio Ghibli soundtracks, partial influence)
- **Notes**: The hirajoshi scale [0, 1, 4, 5, 8] yields these triads. The bII and bVI give the exotic, Asian-influenced sound

### 38. Middle Eastern / Hijaz (i-bII-bIII-iv)
- **Name**: "World: Hijaz / Makam (i-bII-bIII-iv)"
- **Degrees**: i-bII-bIII-iv (phrygian dominant tetrachord)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (phrygian dominant / hijaz mode)
- **Genre**: Middle Eastern, Arabic, Turkish makam
- **Example (D)**: Dm-Eb-F-Gm (D hijaz / D phrygian dominant)
- **Notable Songs**: Traditional Arabic and Turkish music, "Misirlou" (Dick Dale), various Middle Eastern pop
- **Notes**: The Hijaz mode/makam uses the same intervals as the phrygian dominant scale. The augmented 2nd between bII and bIII is the hallmark of Middle Eastern and flamenco music

### 39. Klezmer / Freygish (i-bII-III-VII-i)
- **Name**: "World: Klezmer / Freygish (i-bII-III-VII)"
- **Degrees**: i-bII-III-VII-i
- **Key Mode**: Minor (phrygian dominant / freygish)
- **Genre**: Klezmer, Jewish, Eastern European
- **Example (D)**: Dm-Eb-F-C-Dm
- **Notable Songs**: Traditional klezmer tunes ("Hava Nagila" partial), "Bei Mir Bist Du Schön," various Eastern European folk
- **Notes**: "Freygish" (frigish/freygish) is the Yiddish term for phrygian dominant. The i-bII-III movement is the core of klezmer harmony. The III major chord (not bIII) comes from the phrygian dominant scale

### 40. Dorian Mode (i-IV-i-IV)
- **Name**: "Modal: Dorian Vamp (i-IV)"
- **Degrees**: i-IV-i-IV
- **Key Mode**: Minor (dorian mode)
- **Genre**: Jazz, rock, folk, funk
- **Example (Dm)**: Dm7-G7-Dm7-G7 (or Dm-G-Dm-G for triads)
- **Notable Songs**: "So What" (Miles Davis), "Oye Como Va" (Santana/Tito Puente), "Eleanor Rigby" (Beatles, partial), "Scarborough Fair" (traditional)
- **Notes**: The dorian vamp between i and IV. The raised 6th in dorian (B natural in D dorian) gives a brighter quality than natural minor. Common in jazz and modal jazz

### 41. Dorian / Aeolian Mix (i-bVII-IV)
- **Name**: "Modal: Dorian-Aeolian (i-bVII-IV)"
- **Degrees**: i-bVII-IV
- **Key Mode**: Minor (dorian/aeolian)
- **Genre**: Rock, funk, soul
- **Example (Am)**: Am-G-D
- **Notable Songs**: "Roxanne" (The Police), "Billie Jean" (Michael Jackson, partial), "Thriller" (Michael Jackson, partial)
- **Notes**: Combines the dorian brightness with aeolian; the IV chord is the key dorian characteristic

### 42. Lydian (I-II)
- **Name**: "Modal: Lydian (I-II)"
- **Degrees**: I-II (I to II, both major)
- **Key Mode**: Major (lydian mode)
- **Genre**: Jazz, film, progressive rock
- **Example (C)**: C-D
- **Notable Songs**: "Dreams" (Fleetwood Mac), "Flying in a Blue Dream" (Joe Satriani), film scores (Thomas Newman, John Williams)
- **Notes**: The II chord (major, not diminished) comes from the lydian raised 4th. Creates a floating, ethereal, dreamlike quality

### 43. Whole Tone (I-II or I-III)
- **Name**: "Modal: Whole Tone (I-II-III)"
- **Degrees**: I-II-III (whole tone scale)
- **Key Mode**: Major (whole tone)
- **Genre**: Jazz, impressionist, film
- **Example (C)**: C-D-E (all major or aug)
- **Notable Songs**: Debussy impressionist pieces, "Bemsha Swing" (Thelonious Monk, partial), various film dream sequences
- **Notes**: Based on the whole tone scale. Chords are often augmented. Creates a floating, ambiguous, otherworldly quality

### 44. Mixolydian bVI (I-bVII-bVI-bVII)
- **Name**: "Modal: Mixolydian bVI (I-bVII-bVI-bVII)"
- **Degrees**: I-bVII-bVI-bVII
- **Key Mode**: Major (mixolydian with modal interchange)
- **Genre**: Rock, pop
- **Example (C)**: C-Bb-Ab-Bb
- **Notable Songs**: "Hey Jude" (partial), various rock anthems
- **Notes**: Combines mixolydian bVII with a modal interchange bVI. Creates a rock anthem quality with a dramatic lift

### 45. Spanish / Phrygian (i-bII-i)
- **Name**: "Modal: Phrygian (i-bII-i)"
- **Degrees**: i-bII-i
- **Key Mode**: Minor (phrygian mode)
- **Genre**: Flamenco, metal, progressive rock
- **Example (E)**: Em-F-Em
- **Notable Songs**: "Between the Wheels" (Rush), various metal and prog rock, traditional flamenco
- **Notes**: The simplest phrygian vamp. The bII major chord (F over Em) creates the characteristic dark, tense phrygian sound. The augmented unison/semitone relationship between i and bII is the essence of phrygian

### 46. Spanish Phrygian (i-bII-III)
- **Name**: "Modal: Spanish Phrygian (i-bII-III)"
- **Degrees**: i-bII-III
- **Key Mode**: Minor (phrygian / phrygian dominant blend)
- **Genre**: Flamenco, Spanish
- **Example (Am)**: Am-Bb-C
- **Notable Songs**: Various flamenco forms, "Malagueña"
- **Notes**: Adds the III chord to the phrygian vamp for more harmonic direction. Common in Spanish guitar music

### 47. Blues (I7-IV7-V7)
- **Name**: "Blues: Dominant 7th (I7-IV7-V7)"
- **Degrees**: I7-IV7-V7
- **Key Mode**: Major (with dominant 7ths)
- **Genre**: Blues, rock
- **Example (A)**: A7-D7-E7
- **Notable Songs**: Virtually all blues songs, "Pride and Joy" (Stevie Ray Vaughan), "Red House" (Jimi Hendrix), "Sweet Home Chicago"
- **Notes**: The blues with all dominant 7th chords. The dominant 7th on the I chord (which technically doesn't fit diatonic major) is the defining blues characteristic

### 48. Minor Blues (i7-iv7-V7)
- **Name**: "Blues: Minor Blues (i7-iv7-V7)"
- **Degrees**: i7-iv7-V7
- **Key Mode**: Minor
- **Genre**: Blues, jazz
- **Example (Am)**: Am7-Dm7-E7
- **Notable Songs**: "The Thrill is Gone" (B.B. King), "As The Years Go Passing By," various minor blues tunes
- **Notes**: Minor-key blues; the V7 uses the harmonic minor raised 7th for stronger resolution

## D) Jazz / Sophisticated Progressions

### 49. Rhythm Changes (A Section: I-vi-ii-V)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Rhythm Changes A (I-vi-ii-V)"
- **Degrees**: I-vi-ii-V (repeated)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, bebop
- **Example (Bb)**: Bb7-Gm7-Cm7-F7 (typical with substitutions)
- **Notable Songs**: "I Got Rhythm" (Gershwin), "Oleo" (Sonny Rollins), "Anthropology" (Charlie Parker), "The Flintstones Theme," "Rhythm-a-Ning" (Monk)
- **Notes**: The A section of rhythm changes, one of the two most important progressions in jazz (along with blues). Typically with many substitutions

### 50. Rhythm Changes (B Section: III7-VI7-II7-V7)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Rhythm Changes B (III7-VI7-II7-V7)"
- **Degrees**: III7-VI7-II7-V7 (circle of fifths)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, bebop
- **Example (Bb)**: D7-G7-Cm7-F7 (bridge)
- **Notable Songs**: "I Got Rhythm" bridge, "Oleo" bridge, "Anthropology" bridge
- **Notes**: The B section (bridge) of rhythm changes. Circle of fifths movement through secondary dominants. The most commonly played bridge in jazz

### 51. Coltrane Changes / Giant Steps
- **Name**: "Jazz: Coltrane Changes (Giant Steps)"
- **Degrees**: I-bII-I / movement by major thirds: Bmaj7-D7-Gmaj7-Bb7-Ebmaj7-Am7-Dmaj7 (simplified: I-V/bII-bII ... cycling through 3 key centers a major third apart)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, post-bop
- **Example (Bb)**: Bbmaj7-Eb7-Gbmaj7(Bb/D b)-... (cycles through Bb, Gb/BDb, D keys)
- **Notable Songs**: "Giant Steps" (John Coltrane), "Countdown" (Coltrane), "Lazy Bird" (Coltrane), "Satellite" (Coltrane)
- **Notes**: Root movement by major thirds, creating three key centers an augmented triad apart. Coltrane's harmonic innovation based on dividing the octave into three equal parts. Extremely challenging to improvise over

### 52. Coltrane ii-V-I Substitution (ii-V-I with major third movement)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Coltrane Sub (ii-V-I → ii-V-I in key a major third away)"
- **Degrees**: ii-V-I → (substitute) bvi7-bIII7-bVI7 → V-I
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz
- **Example (C)**: Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 → (becomes) Bbm7-Eb7-Abmaj7-G7-Cmaj7
- **Notable Songs**: "Countdown" (Coltrane), "Tune Up" reharmonization
- **Notes**: The Coltrane substitution inserts a ii-V-I a major third away before resolving back. Creates rapid harmonic motion through distant key areas

### 53. Backdoor (iv-bVII7-I)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Backdoor (iv-bVII7-I)"
- **Degrees**: iv-bVII7-I (or iv7-bVII7-I)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, soul
- **Example (C)**: Fm7-Bb7-Cmaj7
- **Notable Songs**: "Lady Bird" (Tadd Dameron), various standards
- **Notes**: Resolution to I via the "back door" using iv and its dominant bVII7. The bVII7 is the dominant of IV (minor), resolving deceptively. Very common in modern jazz

### 54. Turnaround (I-vi-ii-V)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Turnaround (I-vi-ii-V)"
- **Degrees**: I-vi-ii-V
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, pop
- **Example (C)**: Cmaj7-Am7-Dm7-G7
- **Notable Songs**: End of nearly every jazz standard (the turnaround), "Rhythm Changes" A section
- **Notes**: The standard jazz turnaround. Leads back to the top of the form. Can be played with many substitutions (tritone subs, etc.)

### 55. Minor ii-V-i
- **Name**: "Jazz: Minor ii-V-i"
- **Degrees**: ii°-V-i (or iiø7-V7-i)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (harmonic minor)
- **Genre**: Jazz
- **Example (Am)**: Bm7b5-E7-Am7
- **Notable Songs**: "Autumn Leaves" (sections), "Black Orpheus," "Blue Bossa" (sections)
- **Notes**: The minor key version of ii-V-I. The ii chord is half-diminished (m7b5) and V7 uses the harmonic minor raised 7th. Essential jazz progression

### 56. Bird Blues (Charlie Parker Blues Changes)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Bird Blues"
- **Degrees**: I7-vi7-ii7-V7-... (12-bar form with advanced substitutions, e.g., I-IV-iii-VI-ii-V-I-VI-ii-V-I in Bb)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, bebop
- **Example (Bb)**: Bb7-G7-Cm7-F7-F7-Bb7-G7-Cm7-Cm7-F7-Bb7-G7-Cm7-F7 (simplified Bird changes)
- **Notable Songs**: "Blues for Alice" (Charlie Parker), "An Oscar for Oscar" (Oscar Peterson)
- **Notes**: Charlie Parker's reharmonization of the 12-bar blues, adding ii-V chains and substitutions. The standard form taught in jazz education

### 57. Modal Jazz / So What (i-vi or i-iv vamp)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Modal Vamp (i-iv)"
- **Degrees**: i-iv (or i-vii with modal color)
- **Key Mode**: Minor (dorian)
- **Genre**: Modal jazz
- **Example (Dm)**: Dm7-G7 (or Dm7-Eb7-Dm7 for "So What" vamp)
- **Notable Songs**: "So What" (Miles Davis), "Impressions" (John Coltrane), "Maiden Voyage" (Herbie Hancock)
- **Notes**: Modal jazz uses very few chords, often just a vamp between two chords, allowing extended improvisation on a single scale/mode

### 58. Extended ii-V-I Chain (iii-vi-ii-V-I)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Extended Chain (iii-vi-ii-V-I)"
- **Degrees**: iii-vi-ii-V-I
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz
- **Example (C)**: Em7-Am7-Dm7-G7-Cmaj7
- **Notable Songs**: Many jazz standards, especially as intros and turnarounds
- **Notes**: Extended circle-of-fifths chain. Each chord resolves down a fifth to the next. Creates smooth, continuous harmonic motion toward the tonic

### 59. IV-minor Substitution (IV-iv-I)
- **Name**: "Jazz: IV-iv-I (Minor Subdominant)"
- **Degrees**: IV-iv-I (or IVmaj7-iv7-I)
- **Key Mode**: Major (with borrowed iv from minor)
- **Genre**: Jazz, pop, soul
- **Example (C)**: Fmaj7-Fm6-Cmaj7
- **Notable Songs**: "Night and Day" (Cole Porter), "Charleston" (partial), many jazz standards
- **Notes**: The IV to iv movement is one of the most expressive modal interchange devices. Also called the "minor plagal cadence" when iv resolves directly to I

### 60. Plagal Cadence (IV-I)
- **Name**: "Classical: Plagal (IV-I)"
- **Degrees**: IV-I
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Classical, hymns, rock
- **Example (C)**: F-C
- **Notable Songs**: "Amen" cadence in hymns, many rock and folk endings, "Hey Jude" (ending)
- **Notes**: The "Amen" cadence. Less final than V-I but with a warm, resolved quality. Common in church music and rock endings

### 61. Deceptive Cadence (V-vi)
- **Name**: "Classical: Deceptive (V-vi)"
- **Degrees**: V-vi (or V7-vi)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Classical, pop, jazz
- **Example (C)**: G-Am (instead of G-C)
- **Notable Songs**: Beethoven symphonies, many classical pieces, various pop songs for unexpected resolution
- **Notes**: The V resolves deceptively to vi instead of I. Creates surprise and extends the phrase. One of the most important cadential devices in classical music

### 62. Minor Plagal (iv-I)
- **Name**: "Classical: Minor Plagal (iv-I)"
- **Degrees**: iv-I (borrowed iv from parallel minor)
- **Key Mode**: Major (with borrowed minor iv)
- **Genre**: Pop, jazz, soul
- **Example (C)**: Fm-C
- **Notable Songs**: "Creep" (ending, Radiohead), various soul and R&B songs, Beatles songs
- **Notes**: The minor subdominant resolving to major tonic. Bittersweet, nostalgic quality. Very expressive modal interchange

### 63. Pedal Point (I-IV-I over drone)
- **Name**: "Classical: Pedal (I-IV-I)"
- **Degrees**: I-IV-I (over tonic pedal)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Classical, rock, folk
- **Example (C)**: C-F-C (with C in bass throughout)
- **Notable Songs**: Organ preludes (Bach), rock songs with bass drones, "Tomorrow Never Knows" (Beatles, drone concept)
- **Notes**: Harmonic movement over a static bass note (pedal point). Common in classical organ music and drone-based rock/pop

### 64. Jazz Blues (I7-IV7-I7-vi7-ii7-V7)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Jazz Blues"
- **Degrees**: I7-IV7-I7-vim7-ii7-V7-I7 (12-bar form with bebop additions)
- **Key Mode**: Major (with dominant 7ths)
- **Genre**: Jazz, blues
- **Example (Bb)**: Bb7-Eb7-Bb7-G7-Cm7-F7-Bb7 (simplified)
- **Notable Songs**: "Tenor Madness" (Sonny Rollins), "Blue Monk" (Thelonious Monk), "Freddie Freeloader" (Miles Davis), "Straight, No Chaser" (Monk)
- **Notes**: The jazz blues adds ii-V chains and turnarounds to the basic blues form. The most common jazz blues form includes vim7 in bar 6-7 creating a ii-V turnaround

### 65. Bossa Nova (ii-V-I with extensions)
- **Name**: "Jazz: Bossa Nova (ii-V-I)"
- **Degrees**: ii-V-I (often with extended chords: ii9-V13-Imaj9)
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Bossa nova, Brazilian jazz
- **Example (C)**: Dm9-G13-Cmaj9
- **Notable Songs**: "The Girl from Ipanema" (Jobim), "Desafinado" (Jobim), "Blue Bossa" (Kenny Dorham), "Corcovado" (Jobim)
- **Notes**: Brazilian jazz typically uses ii-V-I with extended chord voicings (9ths, 11ths, 13ths). The rhythm and voicing give it the distinctive bossa sound

### 66. AABA Form (Standard 32-bar)
- **Name**: "Jazz: AABA Form"
- **Degrees**: A: I-vi-ii-V (repeated) / B: III7-VI7-II7-V7 / A: I-vi-ii-V
- **Key Mode**: Major
- **Genre**: Jazz, Broadway, Tin Pan Alley
- **Example (C)**: A: C-Am-Dm-G | C-Am-Dm-G | C-Am-Dm-G | C-Am-Dm-G / B: E7-A7-D7-G7 / A: C-Am-Dm-G
- **Notable Songs**: "I Got Rhythm," "Blue Moon," "Satin Doll" (Ellington), "A Foggy Day" (Gershwin)
- **Notes**: The most important song form in jazz besides blues. 32 bars in AABA structure. Many jazz standards use this form with variations

### 67. ABAC Form (through-composed 32-bar)
- **Name**: "Jazz: ABAC Form"
- **Degrees**: Varies — typically A: 8 bars establishing tonic, B: 8 bars modulating, A: 8 bars restatement, C: 8 bars resolving
- **Key Mode**: Major or minor
- **Genre**: Jazz, Broadway
- **Example (varies)**: e.g., "All The Things You Are" in Ab: Ab-Fm7-Bbm7-Eb7-Ab...
- **Notable Songs**: "All The Things You Are" (Kern), "Autumn in New York" (Vernon Duke), "Cherokee" (Noble)
- **Notes**: ABAC is the other common 32-bar jazz form (besides AABA). Allows for more harmonic exploration than AABA

## Implementation Notes

### Integration with Fretboard App

The progressions are defined using **degree numbers** (1-7) with optional accidentals (b, #) and **quality overrides** (major, minor, dim, aug, "7", maj7, min7, dim7, m7b5), matching the app's existing chord quality system.

**Degree mapping** (major key example — C major):

| Degree | Roman | Root | Diatonic Quality |
|--------|-------|------|-----------------|
| 1 | I | C | major |
| 2 | ii | D | minor |
| 3 | iii | E | minor |
| 4 | IV | F | major |
| 5 | V | G | major |
| 6 | vi | A | minor |
| 7 | vii° | B | dim |

**Borrowed/altered degree notation:**
- `bII` = root is 1 semitone above I (e.g., Db in C major)
- `bIII` = root is 3 semitones above I (e.g., Eb in C major)
- `bVI` = root is 8 semitones above I (e.g., Ab in C major)
- `bVII` = root is 10 semitones above I (e.g., Bb in C major)

**Quality overrides**: Some progressions require specifying the chord quality explicitly (e.g., V7, iv, bII major). The progression data structure should allow each degree to carry an optional quality override. If no override is given, the diatonic quality from the selected scale type is used.

### Suggested Data Structure

```elixir
@progressions [
  %{
    id: "pop_i_v_vi_iv",
    name: "Pop: I-V-vi-IV",
    category: "Famous / Classic",
    degrees: [
      %{degree: 1, quality: nil},        # I — uses diatonic quality
      %{degree: 5, quality: nil},        # V
      %{degree: 6, quality: nil},        # vi
      %{degree: 4, quality: nil}          # IV
    ],
    key_mode: :major,
    genre: "Pop, pop-punk, rock",
    description: "The 'Axis of Awesome' progression used in countless pop hits",
    example_key: "C",
    notable_songs: ["Don't Stop Believin'", "Let It Be", "I'm Yours"]
  },
  # ... etc
]
```

### Suggested Categories (matching scale grouping style)

1. **Famous / Classic** — Pop, rock, blues, classical staples (progressions 1-13)
2. **Jazz & Sophisticated** — ii-V-I, rhythm changes, Coltrane, turnarounds (progressions 49-67)
3. **Curious & Interesting** — Modal interchange, chromatic mediants, Neapolitan (progressions 14-29)
4. **Exotic & World** — Andalusian, phrygian dominant, Hungarian, Japanese, Middle Eastern (progressions 30-48)
5. **Blues** — 12-bar blues forms (progressions 3, 47, 48, 64)

### Quality Compatibility

All chord qualities used in these progressions are supported by the existing `Fretboard.Music.Chord` module:
- `:major`, `:minor`, `:dim`, `:aug`, `:sus2`, `:sus4` (triads)
- `:"7"`, `:maj7`, `:min7`, `:dim7`, `:m7b5` (sevenths)

No new chord qualities need to be added to support any progression in this catalog.
